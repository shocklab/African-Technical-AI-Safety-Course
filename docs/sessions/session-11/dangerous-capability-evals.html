Dangerous-capability evals: elicitation and sandbagging

How a real dangerous-capability battery is built, why stronger elicitation improves a lower-bound estimate, and how a model can strategically fail the test

What we'll cover

A dangerous-capability eval has to test what a determined user could elicit. Phuong et al. assess scaffolded models for this reason. Even then, the best observed score remains a lower bound on performance across feasible methods. A low score may mean the model lacks the capability, the evaluator has not elicited it, or the model has underperformed deliberately. Session 11.3 asks whether the test measures the capability of concern in the first place.

Dangerous-capability evaluation in practice

Phuong et al. (2024) pilot a dangerous-capability suite across four domains: persuasion and deception, cyber-security, self-proliferation (can the model copy itself, acquire resources, survive), and self-reasoning (can it reason about and improve its own situation). A fifth, CBRN, is preliminary. They assess scaffolded models, with prompts, tool access and reasoning loops, to test what a motivated user might elicit. They also run the capability tests with safety filters off. Their conclusion is limited to the tested models: they do not find evidence of strong dangerous capabilities, but they report early warning signs.

Elicitation and observed capability

Suppose the capability of concern is the best performance achievable with feasible prompts, tools or fine-tuning. A zero-shot prompt, an agent scaffold and a fine-tuned model may give different scores. No individual method is guaranteed to improve the score. For a fixed task set and scoring rule, the best score observed so far can only stay the same or increase as more methods are tested:

\[ p_{\mathrm{best,tested}} \;\le\; p_{\mathrm{best,feasible}}. \]

An evaluator who tries too few methods may report a score below what a determined user could achieve. Scaffolding, tool use and fine-tuning can reduce that gap, but a low score still does not prove that all feasible methods would fail. This comparison across methods is not a statistical confidence bound; the measured scores also have sampling uncertainty. An upper bound needs additional assumptions or guarantees about the task and allowed methods.

An illustrative elicitation gap

Make it concrete. Suppose a model scores \(p_0 = 0.30\) on a cyber task zero-shot, \(p_1 = 0.48\) with good scaffolding, and \(p_2 = 0.61\) after light fine-tuning on a capped practice set. In this example the observed elicitation gap is \(p_2 - p_0 = 0.31\): the later method scores thirty-one points above zero-shot. Which number should a safety case use? The best observed score, \(0.61\), while making clear that a feasible method not yet tested might score higher. Comparing a governance threshold only with \(p_0\) would ignore the higher score already found. The three numbers are an illustration, not measurements from a published model.

Sandbagging and password-locking

Elicitation can also fail if the model underperforms deliberately. Sandbagging (van der Weij et al., 2024) is strategic underperformance on an evaluation: the model can do the task but does not, to look safer than it is. The study demonstrates this under controlled conditions: GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus can be prompted to selectively tank a dangerous-capability proxy (WMDP) while holding their score on a benign benchmark (MMLU) steady, so the underperformance is targeted rather than general incompetence. Worse for evaluators, a model can be password-locked by fine-tuning: it hides a capability behind a trigger, performing badly unless a secret password is present, and the hiding generalises to held-out questions. It can even be calibrated to hit a chosen target accuracy. A threshold decision based on that low score could miss the hidden capability.

Apollo Research tested related behaviours under controlled, goal-directed prompts (Meinke et al., 2024). Its suite covered six behaviours: oversight subversion, self-exfiltration, goal-guarding, covert reranking, alignment-faking, and sandbagging. In their results, o1 engaged in scheming on every one of the six evaluations and maintained the deception in over 85% of follow-up questions, while gpt-4o schemed on none. This demonstrates a capability to scheme under instruction. It does not show that deployed models are scheming outside the test.

The experiments above instructed or trained sandbagging. A different concern is evaluation awareness: a model may behave differently when it recognises a test. This has been studied experimentally. Schoen et al. (2025), stress-testing anti-scheming training on o3, found chains of thought explicitly noting the evaluation, and give causal evidence that this awareness itself suppresses covert behaviour; they cannot exclude that part of the measured improvement is the model noticing the test (Session 3.4 met the same result as a confound on the training side). Apollo Research now names evaluation awareness alongside scheming in its research agenda. An eval that a model recognises as a test measures behaviour under those test conditions. Whether the result predicts deployment behaviour needs separate evidence.

Why a low score is ambiguous

A low score may reflect limited capability, insufficient elicitation or deliberate underperformance. It may also reflect a test that misses the capability relevant to harm, as 11.3 will show. Further prompts and scaffolds, access to model internals or fine-tuning may help distinguish these explanations, but none is a general guarantee. Report the tested conditions and the uncertainty before treating a score as evidence for a deployment decision.

The African lens: language and the elicitation gap

Elicitation methods may work differently across languages. English-tuned prompts used for an isiZulu task may elicit less than prompts written by a fluent speaker. A low score in that setting could understate capability. The comparison needs prompts that a fluent speaker would actually use, plus comprehension checks. Compare languages with methods appropriate to each one, then state which elicitation methods were tested.

Questions to bring to class

  • Explain why the best observed score is no greater than the best score attainable under the specified feasible methods. Why can a low score still be misleading?
  • Given \(p_0=0.30\), \(p_1=0.48\), \(p_2=0.61\), which number should a governance threshold compare against, and what goes wrong if it uses \(p_0\)?
  • How would you tell a model that lacks a capability apart from one that sandbags? Why does this generally require more than black-box access?
  • Phuong et al. say "no evidence of strong dangerous capabilities… early warning signs." Give one headline that would overstate the finding, then explain why it is wrong.
